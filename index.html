Section 1

Your one-minute answer

If you only have a minute with the professor, this is what you say. Then learn the three statements you must never mix up.

Say it like this
MediQueue is a web-based outpatient appointment and real-time queue-management prototype. Separately, I built a research experiment that predicts the number of minutes between a patient's physical check-in and the start of their consultation.

For the current model I did not train with the American CDC, English NHS, Brazilian no-show or HospitalOPD data. I generated synthetic outpatient activity with a discrete-event simulator, captured 20 variables available at check-in, and trained a regression model to predict observedWaitMinutes. I compared Gradient Boosting against a historical median and a calibrated queueing rule on independent simulated sessions.

15.941Gradient Boosting MAE, 5,885 held-out simulated visits (min)
34.754Calibrated queue rule MAE (min)
47.553Historical median MAE (min)

The nominal 80% interval covered only 77.45% under standard simulation and 69.57% under stress. So it works as a research demonstration, not as a clinically validated patient-facing estimator.

Three statements you must never mix up

1. A working appointment and queue-management system is not the same thing as a clinically validated prediction system.
2. The HospitalOPD dataset is a real external workflow CSV, but it gave no verified waiting-time target and was not used to fit the Phase 3 model.
3. A 15.941-minute MAE is an average error in simulated minutes. It is not 15.941% accuracy and not a real Nigerian hospital outcome.

Section 2

What problem does MediQueue solve?

Arrive at 9:00 a.m. at an outpatient clinic: you cannot see how many people are ahead, who is consulting, or whether earlier bookings have arrived.

Say it like this
MediQueue handles the operational side: slots, check-in, priority queues, timestamps. The research side asks a narrower question: if I know the queue state when someone checks in, can I estimate how long they will wait before the consultation starts?

Section 3

The precise prediction question

What exactly is the model trying to guess? Get this sentence perfect because the whole defence rests on it.

observedWaitMinutes = consultationStartedAt − checkedInAt (in minutes)
Say it like this
A patient checks in at 9:10 and the clinician starts at 9:42. The observed wait is 32 minutes. It is not the time until the consultation ends, and not the days between booking and the appointment.
Try it: compute the target

Change either time. Notice the result is a duration, which is why this is regression.

Why the outcome is kept out of the inputs

Exam analogy
At 9:10 we know the department, the waiting count, the priority, recent arrivals and staffing. We cannot know when this patient's consultation will start. Putting consultationStartedAt in the inputs is like telling a student the answer before the exam and then claiming they predicted it. That is target leakage.

Regression, not classification

  • Regression predicts a NUMBER, e.g. 32.5 minutes. This is MediQueue.
  • Classification predicts a CATEGORY, e.g. will attend versus will miss.
  • Forecasting predicts a future time-series quantity, e.g. next week's national volume. Not this either.
  • It is not deep learning and not a neural network.
Quick check: which kind of problem?

What is one row?

One completed simulated patient journey: the check-in snapshot plus the later wait. Several rows come from one simulated clinic session, so the train/test split is done by whole sessions (see the split section).

No. It is legitimate for testing systems and controlled operations research, as long as the synthetic origin and generator assumptions are stated and no clinical generalisation is claimed. The 20 input columns (X)

Think of X as 20 clues known at check-in. The model uses the clues to estimate one number y.

Say it like this
X is the 20 information fields available at check-in: queue state, staff capacity, recent activity, time, department and priority. y is observedWaitMinutes. The model predicts y from X.

Tap a column name to see what it means and what to watch for.

The 26 columns at a glance

20input features (X)
5metadata (not predictors)
1target y
26columns in the training CSV
  • Metadata, never fitted: sourceKind, contractVersion, sessionId, scenario, patientSyntheticCode. sessionId is used only to group sessions for splitting.
  • Only departmentCode and priorityAtCheckIn are categorical, so they are one-hot encoded.
  • arrivalHour and arrivalWeekday are replaced by sine and cosine pairs because 23:00 and 00:00 (or Saturday and Sunday) are close on a cycle.
Cyclic encoding: why sine and cosine?

Slide to 23, then to 0. On a plain number line they are 23 apart. On the circle (sine and cosine) they are neighbours.

Tricky questions about the inputs

  • Are the 20 independent? No. waitingAhead equals waitingInDepartment in this contract, and inServiceInDepartment overlaps cliniciansInServiceObserved. Tree models tolerate this, but it affects interpretation.
  • Is activeClinicianShifts the doctors actually attending? No, it is SCHEDULED capacity, not confirmed presence.
  • Do you use name, age or phone? No. No patient identifiers are features and the contract has no age.
  • Do you know the actual wait at check-in? No. It is computed only after consultation starts.

Section 7

How did we generate the synthetic data?

A discrete-event simulation is a virtual clinic that moves forward by events: arrivals, shifts starting, consultations beginning and ending, breaks.

Say it like this
Instead of writing wait = 2 × queue + 10 for every patient, the simulator lets waiting time EMERGE from how arrivals, clinicians, priorities and breaks interact. Then I snapshot 20 fields at check-in and attach the wait that actually happened.
Analogy: the barbershop
A barbershop with three barbers. Customers arrive at random, some haircuts take longer, one barber goes to lunch, a priority booking is served earlier. You cannot get a person's exact wait from the hour alone, but how many are waiting, how many barbers are working and typical haircut length all help. MediQueue's simulator is the healthcare version.
Teaching toy: run a tiny clinic

Drop clinicians to 1 or raise arrivals and watch the line and average wait explode. That is the whole reason queue length alone is not enough.

This is a simplified demonstration of the idea, not your real simulator and not real results. Each tick is one simulated minute. Red dots are urgent/emergency and are served first.

Clinicians
Waiting line (front on the left)

What the real simulator includes

  • Three departments with baseline consultation durations: GOPD 13.5, PAEDIATRICS 17.0, DENTAL 22.0 minutes.
  • Two to five clinicians per session with different shift starts and ends.
  • Arrivals from time-varying Poisson counts; heavier demand early in the clinic day.
  • NORMAL, URGENT, EMERGENCY priorities; a priority queue picks higher-priority waiting cases first.
  • Some booked patients never arrive and never join the physical queue.
  • Consultation length: gamma distribution, hidden patient complexity, doctor/session variation, occasional very long cases.
  • Scheduled breaks, occasional unexpected breaks, staff ending shifts, patients abandoning after long waits.
  • Stress scenario: arrival demand up about 33% and break-duration pressure up about 45%.
Hidden on purpose
The simulator does NOT expose latent patient complexity or future service durations to the model. Those hidden values create realistic uncertainty. It snapshots the 20 permitted fields AT check-in, runs the future, then attaches the observed wait. That makes it an honest supervised-learning task within the chosen assumptions.

Limitations the professor may notice

  • Not calibrated to any named Nigerian hospital.
  • No separate CALLED or SKIPPED states, so those features are always 0.
  • Synthetic shift data is more reliable than real attendance reporting.
  • Patients who abandon before consultation have no wait label and are excluded (possible selection bias).
  • Some test snapshots contain zero scheduled clinician shifts.
  • So training and real operation are not the same population, and results may not transfer.
Section 8

The five comparison methods

Simple to sophisticated. The whole point is to test whether complexity earns its place.

Say it like this
My professor advised comparing methods from simpler to more complex. I tested a historical median, a queue-aware management-science rule, a Decision Tree, KNN, and Gradient Boosting, and chose by validation MAE.
Pick a method

Method A: the median in numbers

Median wait grouped by department and two-hour arrival block, if the group has at least 30 observations; otherwise the overall training median. Median beats mean here because waits have a long right tail.

Mean versus median

Nine people wait 20 minutes. Drag the tenth person's wait. The median refuses to be dragged.

Method B: calibrated queue rule

Workload ahead, plus about half a consultation per busy clinician, divided by scheduled capacity, then calibrated with slope and intercept using training outcomes only. Slopes searched from 0.4 to 2.4; stored values slope 0.85 and intercept 18.017. It is not a full M/M/c model. (Try it in section 6.)

Method E: K-Nearest Neighbours

Find the earlier check-ins most similar to the new one and average their observed waits. No branches, no formula: it predicts from its nearest examples.

Tap the chart.

Bank example: three most similar earlier customers waited 12, 18, 24 minutes, so K = 3 predicts (12 + 18 + 24) / 3 = 18 minutes. MediQueue does the same with 21 neighbours.

How our KNN works, step by step

  1. Past simulated check-ins each have the same 20 fields and a known observedWaitMinutes.
  2. One-hot encode department and priority; fill missing numbers using medians from the TRAINING fold only.
  3. Standardise numeric inputs with training-fold means and standard deviations, so queue length 60 does not dominate hour just because it is bigger.
  4. Find the 21 closest TRAINING check-ins. Never compare against future patients or the held-out fold.
  5. Average their observed waits. A number of minutes, so KNN REGRESSION.
  6. Score with MAE. Repeat for other K inside the development pool and choose K by grouped cross-validation only.
Which K won? (grouped cross-validation MAE)
Tap a point.
  • Selected K = 21 (validation MAE 22.226, the smallest of eight). Fresh test: 22.539 normal, 24.480 stress.
  • Better than the two baselines, worse than the tuned Decision Tree and Gradient Boosting.
  • Examiner trap: KNN is not K-means. KNN is supervised because every neighbour has a known target.
  • Examiner trap: 21 is best for THESE simulated development sessions, not automatically for other data.
Say it like this
Sir, KNN searches for historical queue situations similar to the current check-in. We scaled the inputs, tried eight neighbour counts through four-fold session-grouped validation, and selected K = 21 because it had the lowest validation MAE of 22.226 minutes. On completely new simulated sessions it scored 22.539 minutes, but Gradient Boosting still performed better.

Why split by session, not by row

trainingtesting

Four-fold GroupKFold (the new request)

Not the same thing
The earlier Phase 3 split into train, selection, calibration and test was good practice but it is NOT K-fold cross-validation. The extension implements GroupKFold with four folds.
  • Development pool: the original 160 train + 40 selection sessions = 200 sessions, 20,198 completed observations. Calibration, test and stress sessions were NOT used for tuning.
  • For every candidate setting, train four times: three groups fit, one group validates. No session is ever in both parts of a fold.
  • Average the MAE over validation patients; pick the lowest; refit on the development pool; test ONCE on fresh sessions.
Watch the four folds rotate

Each square stands for 5 of the 200 development sessions. Each fold holds out about 50 sessions.

fit (train)validate
  1. Choose the allowed settings BEFORE looking at the final holdout.
  2. Repeat fit and validation in four whole-session folds. Preprocessing and imputers see only the fitting part.
  3. Select the lowest grouped-CV MAE; every tried setting is recorded in hyperparameter_cv_summary.csv.
  4. Only then evaluate on 55 new normal sessions (5,299 visits) and 55 new stress sessions (5,979 visits).
  5. Do not retune after seeing the fresh test. If you do, you need another new test for a clean claim.
Exam analogy
Study and revise (training). Take several mock tests (cross-validation). Pick the best way of studying. Take the real unseen exam once. The final paper must not be used to decide how you studied.
Parameter versus hyperparameter
A parameter is learned from data during fitting (a split threshold, a leaf value). A hyperparameter is chosen around the fitting (K = 21, max_depth = 10).
Quick check: parameter or hyperparameter?
Check the notes

The sessions are independent, but all come from the same simulator and assumptions. Doing well here does not prove generalisation to real hospitals. Real outpatient data and prospective evaluation are still required.
Section 10

MAE, RMSE and the results

The professor's simplest question will be: what is MAE?

MAE = ( |actual₁ − predicted₁| + … + |actualₙ − predictedₙ| ) / n
Say it like this
MAE is Mean Absolute Error: for each patient, take the difference between actual and predicted wait, ignore the sign, then average. Lower is better. It is in minutes, so 15.941 means about 16 minutes off on average. It is not a percentage accuracy.

  • Median absolute error: the middle absolute error.

Why did Gradient Boosting win?

It sees the whole check-in state and learns nonlinear interactions: ten patients ahead means something different with one busy clinician than with four, and a stressed paediatrics clinic differs from a quiet dental one. The median cannot see live queue conditions; the rule imposes one simple formula.

Coverage: advertised versus actual
Say it like this
The nominal 80% range covered 77.45% on the normal test and only 69.57% under stress. So I report the actual coverage rather than claiming a guaranteed 80%, and I have not enabled live patient predictions.
  • Why only 69.57% under stress? Calibration used standard conditions; stress had higher demand and longer breaks, so the error distribution shifted. Also rows within one session are correlated, so standard exchangeability guarantees do not apply automatically.
  • Is ±25.208 the same for every prediction? Yes (apart from clipping at zero). It is not a personalised uncertainty estimate.
  • Why not show it to patients? Coverage under stress is well below target, evidence is synthetic only, and real-world transfer is unverified.
  • Is 83.33% within ±30 min "84% accuracy"? No. It is a threshold-based descriptive metric, not classification accuracy.
  • The five-method extension did NOT recompute a new interval. The preview still uses the original Phase 3 interval.

Section 13

Data preparation and "Let the Data Speak"

The professor's principle: let the data justify the choices. Clean only where needed, explain every transformation.

  1. Verified every row is labelled SYNTHETIC_RESEARCH_ONLY and matches the Phase 2 contract version.
  2. Checked required columns, valid priority names, legal hours and weekdays, finite non-negative numbers and a non-missing, non-negative wait label.
  3. Protected against leakage: only check-in snapshot values; no future times, labels, identifiers or hidden simulator complexity.
  4. One-hot encoded three departments and three priorities.
  5. Encoded hour and weekday cyclically with sine and cosine.
  6. Imputed early-session missing historical medians using TRAINING statistics only.
  7. Selected on separate validation data and tested on untouched final sessions.
The strongest example
We refused to invent a real waiting-time label from HospitalOPD prescription timestamps.
If asked: "Did you force the data to fit Gradient Boosting?"
No. We predefined the candidates (three originally, five in the extension) and selected by lowest validation MAE. Gradient Boosting happened to win. If the median had won, the correct conclusion would have been that the simpler model was better. I should also say the candidate set is limited and the synthetic generator may favour certain patterns.

Section 14

Connect it to Management Science

Patients are jobs, clinicians are servers, consultation time is service time. A line forms when demand temporarily exceeds capacity.

Say it like this
Management Science asks how to use limited resources efficiently. My model estimates delay; the hospital's managers decide staffing, scheduling and process changes.

Little's Law

L = λ × W (stable system, consistent measurement) L = average number in the system · λ = throughput per time unit · W = average time in the same system
Little's Law calculator

The notes' example: 12 per hour and 1.5 hours gives about 18 people in the process. It does NOT mean any individual waits exactly 90 minutes. The system boundary (whole visit or waiting only) matters.

M/M/c, and why our rule is not M/M/c

M/M/c assumes memoryless Poisson arrivals, exponential service times and c parallel servers. MediQueue has time-varying arrivals, variable consultation lengths, priorities and breaks, so it does not satisfy those assumptions. The calibrated rule is a workload heuristic; Gradient Boosting learns a mapping from simulated queues to waits. Related to queueing research, not an exact M/M/c solution.

A management decision example
If large errors and long waits appear only on Monday mornings with two clinicians, management could adjust rosters, spread check-ins, or add capacity. Prediction alone does not shorten waiting. A claimed improvement would need a before/after or controlled operational study, which has NOT been done.
  • Does a longer queue always mean proportionally longer wait? No: staffing, priorities, consultation complexity, interruptions and arrival timing all matter.
  • Why does the model not assign priority? That needs clinical assessment by qualified staff. Estimating wait is an administrative support task, not triage or diagnosis.
